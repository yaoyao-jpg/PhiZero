PHIZERO INFERENCE PROMPT GUIDANCE
=================================

1. What the prompt should describe
----------------------------------

PhiZero receives two user inputs: a first frame and an English caption. The
first frame establishes the visible subjects, appearance, layout, and initial
state. The caption tells the model how that visible scene begins to evolve.

Write the prompt as a video caption describing visible future motion. It
should not be a command, a list of action keywords, a negative prompt, or a
shot-by-shot script. A good prompt reads like a concise observation of a video:
it identifies the moving subject, states the main action or interaction, and
uses spatial language to make the intended motion unambiguous.

The training-caption specification uses one flowing English sentence or short
paragraph of 25-60 words, written in the present tense. As a practical default,
we recommend one sentence of roughly 25-40 words. The 19 prompts included in
this release contain 26-35 words, with a median of 30 words.

The released case manifest stores only the caption text. The inference code
adds the learned instruction wrapper internally, so a caption should not
include "<image>", "Text caption:", "Predict the motion token sequence", chat
roles, or any other formatting.


2. Recommended pattern
----------------------

Use the following pattern as a guide, not as a rigid form:

  [visible subject anchor]
  + [high-level initiating action or interaction]
  + [manner, speed, and frame-relative direction]
  + [optional immediate visible consequence]
  + [setting or spatial relation]
  + [optional camera, framing, lighting, or visual style]

A copy-ready sentence template is:

  A [visible subject] [primary action] [manner and direction],
  [optional immediate visible consequence], in [setting], while the camera
  [optional camera behavior].

Not every prompt needs every field. The most useful core is:

  visible subject + concrete action + spatial or motion detail

Setting, lighting, color, framing, and camera behavior are supporting details.
Include them when they help identify the scene or control the result, but do
not let static description overwhelm the motion.


3. Detailed writing rules
-------------------------

3.1 Anchor the prompt to a subject visible in the first frame

Name the subject whose motion should be generated. When more than one possible
subject is visible, add a short appearance or position cue:

  A woman in a red jacket ...
  The man standing beside the table ...
  The dog near the wooden fence ...
  A right hand holding a glass cup ...
  The woman on the left ...

Use appearance only to locate the subject. The first frame already supplies
fine visual detail, so there is no need to repeat every item of clothing,
texture, object, or background element.

Avoid an ungrounded pronoun such as "he", "she", or "it" when the first frame
contains several people or objects. For coordinated motion, name the group and
its relationship directly, for example, "Two men carry a wooden box together."


3.2 Describe a concrete, observable action

Prefer verbs that correspond to visible motion:

  walks toward the doorway
  turns her head to the left
  raises his right hand
  bends forward and lifts the bag
  pours batter into the pan
  rolls down the slope and strikes the block

Avoid abstract intent or quality words that do not specify motion:

  moves naturally
  acts energetically
  tries to leave
  behaves realistically
  performs an interesting action

The caption should emphasize the high-level initiating action or interaction.
It may include one immediate visible consequence when that consequence defines
the physical event, but it should not narrate every intermediate state or the
entire final history of the clip.


3.3 Make motion geometry precise

Speed, manner, direction, and spatial relation are often more informative than
generic style adjectives. Useful expressions include:

  slowly, quickly, gently, steadily, repeatedly
  upward, downward, forward, backward
  from the left side of the frame to the right
  toward the camera, away from the camera
  toward the doorway, across the room, along the street
  into the bowl, beside the plate, near the center of the floor

For left and right, prefer frame-relative language when possible. "Moves from
the left side of the frame to the right" is clearer than "moves right". If the
direction belongs to a person rather than the image plane, state the relation,
for example, "turns toward the person standing on her left."


3.4 Describe interactions through visible cause and effect

For hand-object interaction, collision, deformation, fluid motion, or another
physical event, state what contacts or acts on what. A concise immediate result
can remove ambiguity:

  A hand lifts the cup and places it beside the plate.
  A red cart strikes a blue cart, pushing it backward.
  Thick batter pours into the pan and spreads outward.
  Green slime sags at the edges and forms a wider shape.

Describe what a viewer can see rather than naming an abstract law or asking the
model to "simulate physics." For example:

  Less useful: The ball obeys gravity and conservation of momentum.
  Better:     The ball falls, strikes the floor, and bounces upward.

Use result clauses selectively. The training captions prioritize the initiating
action over exhaustive temporal evolution. "The bottle pours liquid onto the
charcoal, causing the flames to surge upward" is useful; a frame-by-frame list
of every flame shape is not.


3.5 Keep temporal structure short and coherent

Words such as "while", "as", "then", "before", and "after" can distinguish
simultaneous and sequential motion:

  while / as       simultaneous events
  then             one short follow-up action
  before / after   an explicit order when the order matters
  continues        sustained motion
  repeatedly       repeated motion

For the released 33-frame, 8-FPS setting, describe one main event or at most a
few tightly connected actions. Prefer a unified caption over a numbered list or
timeline. "A woman lifts the cup, takes a sip, and lowers it toward the table"
is appropriate; a long story containing unrelated actions and scene changes is
not.


3.6 Distinguish subject motion from camera motion

Camera behavior is optional. If it matters, make "the camera" the explicit
subject of the camera clause:

  The camera slowly pans to the right.
  The camera tracks the running man from the side.
  The camera moves forward along the street.
  The camera remains fixed in a steady medium shot.

Avoid ambiguous wording such as "the scene moves left." State whether an object
moves within the frame or whether the camera moves:

  The cyclist moves from right to left while the camera remains fixed.
  The camera pans left to follow the cyclist.

Do not request mutually incompatible camera behavior, such as a fixed camera
that also pans left.


3.7 Use scene, framing, light, color, and style as supporting context

The training captions include observable setting, camera/framing, lighting,
colors, and visual style when useful. Short phrases are usually sufficient:

  in a softly lit kitchen
  on a sunlit city street
  against a blurred green background
  in a steady overhead close-up
  captured in a fixed wide shot
  in slow-motion footage

Keep the expected action as the grammatical center of the sentence. A prompt
that only inventories static appearance does not provide a motion condition.


3.8 Stay consistent with the first frame

The caption and first frame should refer to the same visible scene. Do not
introduce a different main character, object, location, or initial position
unless its entrance is itself the intended visible event. Do not describe a
subject as standing on the left if it is clearly on the right.

The first frame controls appearance more directly than text. Use the caption to
specify motion and interaction, not to redesign the target image. For motion
transfer, describe the source motion using subject-neutral language when the
target has a different embodiment; preserve the motion geometry and interaction
rather than insisting on source-specific identity details.


4. Strong prompt examples
-------------------------

Human motion:

  A woman in a red jacket walks steadily from the left side of the frame toward
  the doorway, reaching for its handle in a softly lit room while the camera
  remains fixed.

Hand-object interaction:

  In a steady close-up, a right hand lifts the blue cup from the table and moves
  it beside the plate, with warm indoor light reflecting across the glass
  surface.

Coordinated motion:

  Two men carry a wooden box together from the back of the room toward the
  foreground, keeping it level as a side-tracking camera follows their
  coordinated movement.

Collision:

  A red cart rolls quickly from left to right and strikes a blue cart near the
  center of the floor, pushing it backward in a fixed wide shot.

Fluid motion:

  Thick pancake batter pours from a bowl into the center of a heated pan,
  spreading outward into a wider circular pool in a steady overhead close-up.

Deformation:

  A translucent block of green slime slowly spreads across the plastic sheet,
  sagging at the edges and forming a wider, flatter shape while the camera
  remains fixed.

Camera-aware motion:

  A cyclist rides steadily down the sunlit street away from the viewer while
  the camera tracks forward behind them, keeping the rider centered in a
  medium shot.

Object release:

  A hand releases a small glass ball above the wooden floor, allowing it to fall
  straight downward and bounce near the center of a steady close-up view.


5. Common failure patterns and revisions
----------------------------------------

Keyword list:

  Avoid:  woman, walking, doorway, camera motion
  Use:    A woman walks steadily toward the doorway while the camera remains
          fixed in a medium shot.

Command language:

  Avoid:  Make the woman walk naturally and keep everything realistic.
  Use:    A woman walks slowly across the room toward the open doorway in a
          steady full-body shot.

Abstract physics:

  Avoid:  Simulate gravity and conservation of momentum realistically.
  Use:    A metal ball falls onto the floor, rebounds upward, and settles near
          the point of impact in a fixed close-up.

Ambiguous motion:

  Avoid:  The person moves over there while the scene shifts.
  Use:    The person walks from the right side of the frame toward the table as
          the camera pans left to follow.

Overwritten storyline:

  Avoid:  First she enters, then finds a cup, fills it, talks to another person,
          crosses the room, opens a window, and finally sits down.
  Use:    A woman enters from the left, walks toward the table, and reaches for
          a cup while the camera holds a steady wide view.

Static-only description:

  Avoid:  A beautiful woman in a detailed red jacket stands in a luxurious room
          with cinematic lighting and rich colors.
  Use:    A woman in a red jacket turns away from the window and walks toward
          the center of the warmly lit room in a steady medium shot.


6. Formats to avoid
-------------------

Do not use:

  - isolated keywords or tag lists;
  - imperative instructions such as "make", "generate", or "animate";
  - numbered shots, timestamps, per-frame coordinates, JSON, or XML;
  - a long chain of unrelated events or multiple scene changes;
  - mostly static appearance with no future action;
  - abstract intentions or physical-law names without visible motion;
  - long lists of negative constraints;
  - contradictory subject or camera directions;
  - content that conflicts with the supplied first frame;
  - Chinese, mixed Chinese-English, or instruction/chat formatting.


7. Final checklist
------------------

Before inference, check that the prompt:

  [ ] is written in English and present tense;
  [ ] is one flowing caption, normally 25-40 words and within 25-60 words;
  [ ] identifies a subject visible in the first frame;
  [ ] states a concrete high-level action or interaction;
  [ ] gives useful direction, speed, manner, or spatial relations;
  [ ] includes only a concise visible consequence when one is needed;
  [ ] distinguishes subject motion from camera motion;
  [ ] uses setting and visual details only as supporting context;
  [ ] remains consistent with the first frame; and
  [ ] contains no command syntax, timeline, token instruction, or negative list.

If these conditions are met, the prompt is close to the caption distribution
used to train PhiZero's Physical Language Reasoner.
